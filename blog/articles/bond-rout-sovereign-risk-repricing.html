The Bond Rout That Repriced the World

22 August 2026

The number that changes everything

The US 30-year Treasury yield ended at 5.23% on August 20, after briefly touching 5.337% on August 18 — the highest in 19 years. The 10-year sits at 4.69%. But the number that matters most isn't either of those yields in isolation. It's the relationship between them and the S&P 500's earnings yield: bond yields are now higher than equity earnings yields. That hasn't happened in two decades, and it forces a genuine rethink of where capital goes next.

The intervention that didn't work

Treasury Secretary Bessent announced on August 19 that he would at least double the size of buyback operations for 10-30 year securities — a direct intervention in the long end of the curve. The 30-year yield briefly fell to 5.19%. Then it rebounded. By August 21, it was back at 5.23%. JPMorgan's James Sullivan called it a move that merely "shifts the problem down the road" — the Treasury is buying back long-dated bonds while issuing shorter bills, leaving the underlying debt burden intact.

Bessent says he could increase buybacks further. But the market's verdict is clear: this is a band-aid on a structural wound. The national debt formally crossed $40 trillion this week, with annual debt servicing costs at $1.17 trillion. The Treasury is trying to manage a yield curve while the fiscal picture deteriorates underneath it.

What's driving the rout

The sell-off isn't one story. It's three converging:

  • US fiscal deterioration. FY2026 deficit already surpassing 2025 at ~$1.8 trillion. Debt servicing at $1.17T on $39.9T debt. Markets pricing no rate cut for five years. The government can't stimulate aggressively, and the Fed can't cut without spiking yields further.
  • Record corporate supply. AI-related companies are estimated to issue as much as $1.5 trillion in bonds this year. Hyperscalers competing for capital with the US Treasury itself — HSBC's Willem Sels identified this as "the key concern currently."
  • Oil inflation. Brent at $93.87, six straight up days, 7% weekly gain. Diesel at record crack spreads. Energy costs feeding directly into breakeven inflation expectations, pushing real yields higher.

It's not just the US

France's 30-year yield hit its highest since 2011. Japan's 10-year JGB reached 2.90%, the highest since 1996, with the 30-year at a record 3.97%. The BOJ is expected to hike in September — an 80% probability — which would be the second hike in three months. The bond rout is global, driven by the same forces: fiscal deterioration, energy inflation, and central banks that can't ease without abandoning their inflation mandates.

Japan's situation is particularly acute. The Takaichi government's food tax cut is adding fiscal pressure at exactly the moment the BOJ is trying to normalize rates. The 10-year JGB at 2.90% is repricing the entire Japanese yield curve, with implications for the $1.8 trillion carry trade that has relied on Japanese rates staying near zero.

The asset allocation shift

When bond yields exceed equity earnings yields, the calculus for every portfolio changes. For two decades, the answer to "bonds or stocks?" was almost always stocks — bonds paid nothing, so you took the equity risk premium. Now the 30-year at 5.23% offers a meaningful return with none of the equity drawdown risk. PBS: mortgage rates heading toward 7%. Auto loans, credit cards, corporate borrowing costs — all repricing upward.

The S&P 500 hit an intraday record of 7,816.70 on August 13, then snapped a three-week winning streak, closing at 7,674 on August 21. Nine of 11 sectors were negative. The pullback isn't driven by earnings weakness — Walmart beat on EPS and revenue. It's driven by yields dictating equity direction: stocks fall when yields rise, rise when yields fall. The bond market is now the tail that wags the equity dog.

The Jackson Hole test

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh delivers his first keynote at Jackson Hole on August 28 — 19 days before the September 16 FOMC decision. He has signaled he is "not constrained by market prices", a hawkish posture that suggests he won't bend to political pressure or market expectations. September hike odds sit at ~33%.

The risk runs asymmetrically. A hawkish message reprices rate-sensitive equities and pushes long bonds even higher. A dovish surprise triggers a sharp dollar decline and bond rally — but that rally would undermine Bessent's buyback credibility. Warsh has reduced post-meeting statements to ~130 words, declined to submit a dot plot projection, and initiated discussion about reducing FOMC meetings from 8 to 6 per year. He is deliberately making the Fed less predictable. That itself is a source of volatility.

What it means

The bond market is repricing sovereign risk globally. This isn't a temporary dislocation — it's the market absorbing the reality that governments borrowed heavily when money was cheap, and the bill is now coming due. The US, France, Japan — all facing the same structural squeeze: debt servicing costs rising while growth slows and energy costs climb.

The buyback intervention signals official concern, but JPMorgan's critique shows markets don't believe it addresses the root cause. The 30-year at 5.23% isn't just a number. It's the market saying: the era of cheap money is over, and the governments that relied on it are now competing with the private sector for a shrinking pool of willing lenders. The next test comes in 48 hours, when Nvidia reports earnings and Warsh steps to the podium. Two seismic inputs, 36 hours apart, will define whether this rout stabilizes or accelerates.
