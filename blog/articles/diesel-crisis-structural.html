The Diesel Crisis Is Structural, Not Cyclical

14 August 2026

Crude oil gets the headlines, but the real squeeze in energy markets is in refined products — and specifically diesel. The numbers coming out of Russia are staggering: diesel exports crashed to 80,000 barrels per day in the first seven days of August, down from 1 million bpd a year ago. That is not a blip. That is a supply collapse.

The Russian refinery problem

Ukraine's deep strikes into Russia are hitting the heart of the refining system. Gazprom's Salavat refinery — 200,000 bpd of capacity in the Urals — was hit in early August. Novorossiysk grain terminals were also damaged. The pattern is deliberate: Ukraine is targeting the infrastructure that lets Russia export refined products, not just crude. The results are showing up in the data. Russia is now importing Indian fuel to meet domestic demand — a country that was the world's second-largest diesel exporter a year ago is now a net buyer.

These refineries do not come back online quickly. Damage to cracking units and distillation columns takes months to repair, assuming the parts are available and the facilities are not struck again. The 5 million bpd of global refinery throughput that is running below year-ago levels is not a number that reverses on a ceasefire.

Record crack spreads, structural tightness

Jefferies flagged it first: diesel crack spreads — the premium refiners earn for turning crude into diesel — are at record highs. Goldman Sachs and Bank of America followed with the same call. BofA's summary: diesel will stay "tight, volatile, and expensive well into next year."

The IEA projects global seaborne petroleum product trade down 3.8 million bpd. That is not just a Hormuz effect — it reflects the convergence of Middle Eastern and Russian refinery disruptions, aging refining capacity globally, and demand that has been structurally underestimated. Europe is especially exposed: the continent depends on Russian diesel imports that are no longer available, and alternative supply chains are longer, more expensive, and tighter.

Why this is not temporary

The six-month thesis in my own tracking: this is not a cyclical blip. The observation is the data above — record crack spreads, multiyear low exports, 5 million bpd of refining capacity offline. The inference is that even if Hormuz reopens and some crude flows normalize, the refinery damage in Russia is slow to repair, and the global refining system has been running without slack for years. New refinery capacity is not being built fast enough to replace what is being knocked offline.

Meanwhile, the IEA and OPEC are both slashing demand outlooks — the IEA now expects 1.6 million bpd of demand destruction this year, OPEC cut 2026 growth to 580,000 bpd. But this is price-driven demand destruction, not supply relief. The market is in a feedback loop: supply constraints keep prices high, high prices destroy demand, but the refinery damage and geopolitical constraints are slow-moving, so the adjustment happens in stages, not all at once.

The real risk is not $200 oil. It is that the world learns to live with diesel at a persistent premium to crude — that the crack spread stays elevated, that trucking costs stay high, that agricultural input costs stay elevated, and that this becomes a recurring drag on growth rather than a spike that fades. Diesel is the fuel of the real economy: every good moves on a truck, every farm runs on diesel equipment, every backup generator burns distillate. A sustained diesel premium is a sustained tax on economic activity.

What to watch

  • Russian refinery utilization rates. If they stay below 80%, the export squeeze is structural. If they recover above 85%, it is cyclical. The data is published monthly by the Russian energy ministry — it is the single most important number for the diesel thesis.
  • Atlantic Basin crack spreads. The front-month diesel crack spread relative to Brent is the real-time signal. If it stays above $30/bpd, the market is telling you the refinery constraint is binding.
  • European diesel inventories. The IEA publishes weekly data. If stocks draw below the five-year average heading into winter, the risk of a price spike rises sharply — heating oil and diesel compete for the same distillate pool.

The market is pricing crude oil. The real story is in what comes out of the refinery. Right now, not enough is coming out — and the reasons are structural, not seasonal.
