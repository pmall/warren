The Second Chokepoint — Houthis Take Bab el-Mandeb

12 September 2026

In an 18-hour blitz that ended September 11, Yemen's Houthis captured Perim Island — also known as Mayun — along with the ports of Mokha and Dhubab, completing full control of the Bab el-Mandeb strait. Government forces withdrew. More than 2,000 troops remain besieged on the Hanish Islands. Euronews confirmed the island fell on September 11, a day after Mokha was taken.

The world's two most important energy chokepoints are now both under Iranian-allied control.

From Port to Strait

The Houthi campaign began September 8 with ballistic missile and drone attacks on four Saudi cities — Abha, Khamis Mushait, Jazan, and Najran. Seventy-three wounded. The Jazan refinery, 400,000 barrels per day of capacity, targeted again. The Saudi-led coalition vowed a "firm response."

But the real operation was elsewhere. While Saudi cities burned, Houthis moved on the Yemeni Red Sea coast. Mokha fell September 10. Perim Island — the rock in the middle of the strait that gives whoever holds it control over every vessel passing through — fell the next morning. Dhubab and the coastal village of Murad followed. Government forces, outgunned and outmaneuvered, withdrew entirely.

Perim Island is the strategic prize. At roughly 130 square kilometers, it sits directly in the shipping lane. Whoever holds Perim can observe, threaten, or stop any vessel transiting Bab el-Mandeb. The Houthis now hold it.

The Dual-Chokepoint World

Since February, the Hormuz crisis has been the defining story of the energy war. Iran declared the strait "completely closed" in August, then transitioned to a managed-filtering model: an exclusion zone, a regulatory framework, an Oman corridor in its "final stage." Transit volume remains in single digits — seven vessels on September 11 versus a pre-conflict average of 129 per day. The strait carries roughly 7% of what it used to.

Bab el-Mandeb was always the second pressure point. Houthi threats to Red Sea shipping forced tankers to reroute around the Cape of Good Hope, adding weeks and cost. But the strait remained technically open — vessels transited, even if at reduced volume and elevated risk.

That is no longer the case. With Perim Island in Houthi hands, the strait is fully controlled by a force aligned with Iran. The Houthis told the Associated Press there was "no need for international concern" but added that Saudi Arabia remained an exception. That exception is the point: the Houthis can selectively allow or deny transit, the same model Iran uses at Hormuz.

The dual-chokepoint squeeze is now structural, not theoretical. Hormuz restricts what leaves the Persian Gulf. Bab el-Mandeb restricts what moves through the Red Sea. Together, they govern the flow of roughly 20 million barrels per day of crude and refined products — or they did, before the war.

What Ships Do Now

The immediate question is routing. Vessels heading from the Persian Gulf to Europe and North America through the Suez Canal must pass both chokepoints. Those heading to Asia via the Red Sea must clear Bab el-Mandeb. The options are narrowing.

CENTCOM redirected 96 vessels on September 11 alone as the Houthi capture became clear. The Houthis expanded their prohibited zone to include the Gulf of Oman and Arabian Sea — the first extension beyond the strait itself. The message: the control perimeter is growing, not shrinking.

For crude flowing from the Persian Gulf to Western markets, the routing options are now severely constrained. The SUMED pipeline through Egypt offers an alternative to Hormuz for loading at the Red Sea end, but Bab el-Mandeb blocks the path from the Red Sea to the Mediterranean. The Cape of Good Hope reroute adds 10 to 14 days and millions in fuel and insurance costs. Neither option solves the fundamental problem: the two straits that anchor global energy shipping are both under hostile control.

Oil and the Market

Brent settled at $107.63 on September 10, then pulled back to $104.42 on September 12 on signals of Oman-brokered diplomacy between Iran and Gulf states. The pullback snapped a five-day Brent rally but left prices up 8.96% for the week.

The market is still pricing the Hormuz disruption more heavily than Bab el-Mandeb. That may be wrong. Hormuz carries crude — Bab el-Mandeb carries crude and refined products. With US diesel at a record $5.90 per gallon and crack spreads at all-time highs, the refined-products chokepoint may matter more to consumers than the crude chokepoint.

Goldman Sachs sees Brent above $120 if 4 million barrels per day of outages persist. The dual-chokepoint configuration makes that scenario more likely, not less. Every vessel diverted from the Red Sea tightens the Atlantic Basin diesel market further. Heating season begins next month.

The Saudi Bind

Saudi Arabia is caught. The kingdom's southern cities are under Houthi fire. Its largest refinery near the Yemen border has been struck repeatedly. Its crude exports have fallen to 3 million barrels per day — a nine-year low — as both Persian Gulf and Red Sea routes face pressure.

A military response against Yemen risks escalation with Iran at the worst possible moment. Iran called for an end to the Saudi blockade of Yemen and backed the Houthis after the Perim capture. The diplomatic framing — Iran as patron, Houthis as proxy — makes any Saudi strike on Yemen a potential trigger for broader conflict.

Trump rejected MBS's request for strikes on Houthi positions. The White House, per the Wall Street Journal, has discussed the possibility of the conflict dragging beyond Trump's term. The Atlantic reported that the Houthis are now the second major chokepoint under Iranian-allied control — a fact that no amount of rhetoric can undo.

What It Means

The energy war has entered a new phase. For seven months, the question was whether Hormuz would reopen. Now the question is whether any major energy shipping route remains uncontrolled by a hostile force.

Two chokepoints. Two sets of hostile control. One regulated by a fee and exclusion zone, the other by military seizure. The global energy shipping system was built on the assumption that these straits would remain open under international law. That assumption is gone.

The Houthis told the AP there was "no need for international concern." But Perim Island is in their hands, and the strait it commands carries 10% of the world's seaborne oil trade. The concern is not needed — it is already priced.
