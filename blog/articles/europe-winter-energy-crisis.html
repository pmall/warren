Europe's Winter Is Two Crises at Once

17 August 2026

Europe is heading into winter with gas inventories at a 17-year low and a drought that has dried up rivers, cut nuclear output, and burned half a million hectares of forest. Either one alone would be a serious problem. Together, they compound — the drought reduces hydropower and forces thermal plants to burn more gas, while the gas supply is tighter than it was during the 2022 crisis. The continent is walking into a dual energy shock with fewer buffers than it had four years ago.

Gas: tighter than 2022, and the ban is self-defeating

European gas storage is at its lowest level heading into September since the mid-2000s. Prices are twice pre-war levels. The EU banned Russian pipeline gas after the 2022 invasion, and a ban on Russian LNG imports takes effect in January 2027. But the data tells a different story: EU purchases of Russian LNG hit an all-time high this year. Belgium sourced 100% of its gas from Russia in July. Traders are front-loading purchases ahead of the ban, which means inventories are being filled with the very supply the EU says it wants to eliminate.

The result is a policy contradiction that the market is pricing: the ban creates urgency to buy Russian LNG now, which tightens the market further, which raises prices, which makes the eventual switch to non-Russian supply more expensive. Governments may force traders to buy now to fill storage before winter, which would trigger a bidding war with Asia — where demand is rising as China and India compete for the same LNG cargoes.

New US LNG export capacity has come online, but it is not enough to cover the gap this winter. Winter gas consumption in Europe rises roughly twofold compared to summer. With inventories already low and the summer heatwave driving above-average cooling demand, the buffer heading into the heating season is thin. If a cold snap hits early, the market will have no margin.

The drought makes everything worse

The European drought is not a separate problem from the gas crisis — it is a force multiplier. The Rhine at Cologne is at 24 centimeters, well below the 40-centimeter minimum for barge navigation. That means coal and gas shipments by river — the backbone of German energy logistics — are disrupted or impossible. The Po River in Italy has dried up, with seawater intruding upstream. The Danube is at a 90-year low.

Rivers are not just transport routes. They are cooling water sources for nuclear plants and inputs for hydropower. France power prices jumped 22% as heatwave conditions trimmed nuclear output. Romania shut its last Danube nuclear reactor. Hydropower generation is below normal across southern Europe. Every megawatt-hour lost from nuclear and hydro has to come from somewhere — and that somewhere is gas-fired generation, which pushes demand higher into an already tight supply picture.

Forty-three percent of French watercourses are dry or disrupted. The EU wildfire burnt area is at 505,683 hectares year-to-date, already above 2025, which was the worst on record. Fifty percent of the EU and UK are under drought conditions at some level. The Joint Research Centre says conditions are "worsening" with continued warm and dry weather through September. El Niño may extend these conditions into spring 2027.

The compounding effect

The danger is not either crisis in isolation — it is the feedback loop between them. Low river levels mean less hydropower, which means more gas burned for electricity, which draws down storage faster, which raises prices, which makes the drought's economic damage worse because energy costs rise for agriculture, transport, and industry. Meanwhile, the drought itself is reducing agricultural output, which adds food inflation to energy inflation.

Europe experienced this compounding in 2022, when the gas crisis and a summer drought hit simultaneously. The difference now is that inventories are lower, the Russian supply is more restricted, and the drought is more severe. The continent has less room to maneuver than it did four years ago, and it is heading into the same season with fewer options.

The EU's energy system was designed for a world where one crisis at a time was the expectation. Two concurrent crises — one geopolitical, one climatic — stress the system in ways it was not built for. The question heading into winter is not whether Europe will have enough energy. It will, at a price. The question is what that price does to an economy already contending with high inflation, slow growth, and a drought that is eating into output across the continent.

What to watch

  • Storage fill rate. The IEA and Gas Infrastructure Europe publish weekly storage data. If inventories are below 80% full by October 1 — the typical threshold for comfortable winter supply — the risk of a price spike rises sharply.
  • Rhine water levels. The German Federal Waterways and Shipping Administration publishes daily Rhine gauge readings. If levels stay below 40 centimeters through September, coal and gas logistics remain disrupted heading into peak demand.
  • Asian LNG buying. Chinese and Indian LNG importers are competing for the same cargoes. If Asia buys aggressively to fill its own storage, Europe loses the supply margin it needs. Platts and ICIS track spot LNG cargo flows.
  • Cold snap timing. An early cold snap in October or November — before storage is fully filled — would force the market to price in scarcity immediately. The European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts publishes seasonal outlooks.

Europe beat the 2022 gas crisis through a mild winter, aggressive LNG buying, and demand reduction. It enters 2026 with less supply, less storage, and a drought that is adding pressure from a direction the energy system was not designed to handle. The winter ahead is not a replay of 2022. It is something Europe has not faced before: two energy shocks arriving at the same time, each making the other worse.
