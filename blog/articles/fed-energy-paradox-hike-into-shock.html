The Fed's Energy Paradox — Hiking Into a Supply Shock

11 September 2026

Brent settled at $107.63 on Wednesday, up 6.3% in the biggest single-day jump since the Iran war began. WTI hit $102.48. Both are back above $100 for the first time since May. Diesel is at $5.90 a gallon — an all-time record. The CPI print for August arrives at 8:30 AM Eastern today, and CME FedWatch prices a 70% probability that the Fed will hike rates on September 17.

Here is the problem: rate hikes make this worse.

The Dollar Channel Runs Backward

The standard logic of a rate hike is straightforward — higher rates attract capital, strengthen the dollar, and cool demand. In a demand-driven inflation scenario, that works. But the current inflation is not demand-driven. It is a supply shock. The Strait of Hormuz is carrying six vessels a day against a typical 129. Iran's IRGC struck a US drone at the entrance on September 10 and expanded the prohibited zone to the Gulf of Oman and Arabian Sea. War-risk insurance is 40 to 60 times baseline. CENTCOM redirected 96 commercial vessels.

When you hike into a supply shock, the dollar rises, and oil — priced in dollars globally — becomes more expensive for every buyer using another currency. The stronger dollar transmits the rate hike into higher energy costs worldwide. That higher energy cost feeds back into US consumer prices through gasoline, diesel, and heating oil. The Fed's tool for fighting inflation becomes a mechanism for importing more of it.

The Diesel Trap

The inflation the Fed is trying to fight is most visible in diesel. US average diesel hit $5.90 a gallon — up 56% year-over-year. California is at $7.70. US refineries are running at 98% utilization, the highest since August 2018. Global refinery capacity offline sits near 5 million barrels per day — a combination of Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries and Middle East disruptions.

Diesel is the fuel of the economy. Every good in America moves by truck, rail, or ship — all diesel-powered. When diesel hits $5.90, the cost moves through every supply chain, every shelf price, every service. The Fed can raise rates to cool demand for mortgages and credit cards, but it cannot raise rates to fix a refinery running at 98% with nowhere to add capacity. The bottleneck is physical, not financial.

The CPI Print as Inflection Point

August CPI is expected at +0.4% month-over-month and 3.4% year-over-year on the headline number. Core is expected at +0.4% MoM and 2.4% YoY. The PPI released Wednesday came in in line at +0.4% MoM and 5.4% YoY, with energy up 4.2% MoM.

If CPI comes in hot, the 70% hike probability becomes near-certain. JPMorgan has already revised its base case to a 25-basis-point September hike. Barclays expects two hikes in 2026. Citigroup pushed its next rate cut forecast to June 2027.

But the CPI print itself is partly a lagging indicator. It reflects July and early August energy prices — before Brent surged past $107. The real inflation impulse from the Sep 10 oil spike hasn't hit the CPI yet. A hot print today confirms the backward-looking story. The forward-looking story is worse.

Equities Are Already Pricing the Squeeze

The S&P 500 closed at 7,591.75 on Wednesday, down 0.58% — its fourth straight loss and now roughly 3% below its August peak of 7,816.70. The 10-year yield hit 4.963%, the highest since 2023. Nine of 11 sectors closed red. The index is now at 19 times forward earnings — the cheapest since April 2025.

The market is doing what it does when the Fed is trapped — it reprices risk. Higher yields compete with equities for capital. Higher energy costs compress margins. A 70% hike probability means the discount rate on future earnings is about to rise. The only sector holding up is energy, and that is the inflation itself.

The Paradox Has No Clean Exit

The Fed's dilemma is structural. If it holds, it signals that energy-driven inflation is tolerable, which anchors inflation expectations higher and risks a wage-price spiral through diesel's pass-through to every good and service. If it hikes, it strengthens the dollar, which raises the dollar price of oil globally, which feeds back into the domestic CPI the Fed is trying to cool.

Goldman Sachs sees Brent above $120 if 4 million bpd of outages persist. The Hormuz crisis is in its seventh month with no diplomatic off-ramp visible. The Houthis just attacked four Saudi cities. IRGC fired 20 missiles at Jordan. Gold plunged $100 to $4,319, breaking its 200-day EMA — the safe haven that usually benefits from exactly this kind of chaos is now trading on rate-hike probability instead of risk-off demand.

The CPI print will confirm what everyone already knows: inflation is here and it is driven by energy supply, not demand. The Fed will hike anyway, because Warsh's credibility now depends on it after Jackson Hole. And the hike will make the energy cost problem marginally worse through the dollar channel. That is the paradox. There is no clean exit. There is only the next variable — and after today's CPI, the only remaining question is whether 25 basis points in September will be the last hike or the first of several.
