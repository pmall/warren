Five Percent — The Bond Market's Verdict on the New Regime

15 September 2026

The 10-year Treasury yield touched 5.014% intraday Monday — a level that hasn't held since 2007. It pulled back to 4.95% by the close, but the breach is the message: the bond market just told the Fed what it thinks of the next 24 hours.

The number that matters

Five percent on the 10-year is not just a round number. It is the threshold where debt service costs become self-reinforcing. The U.S. is carrying $40 trillion in debt and paying $1.17 trillion a year to service it. At 5%, every new issuance costs more to roll, and the deficit — already $1.8 trillion for fiscal 2026 — widens further. The bond market is pricing a world where the government cannot afford to stimulate and the Fed cannot afford to cut.

The 30-year hit 5.42%, a 19-year high. Japan's 10-year JGB breached 3% for the first time since 1996. The UK 10-year reached 5.38%, highest since 2008. This is not a U.S. story. It is a global repricing of sovereign risk, and the trigger is oil.

Why now

Three forces converged in the same week. First, the Saudi East-West pipeline — the primary bypass for Hormuz — remains shut after drone strikes from Iraq's Maysan province on September 11-12. It was carrying 4 to 5 million barrels per day. Yanbu stocks can sustain exports for five to seven days, putting exhaustion around September 18-19. The pipeline was the last insurance policy against a full Hormuz closure. Now there is none.

Second, CME FedWatch surged to 92.4% for a 25-basis-point hike Wednesday. Goldman Sachs, J.P. Morgan, HSBC, Deutsche Bank, and Morgan Stanley all now forecast a hike. J.P. Morgan also expects a second hike in December and raised its long-run policy rate estimate to 3.25%. The Fed is about to raise rates into the weakest consumer sentiment since the University of Michigan survey began — 47.8 in September preliminary, year-ahead inflation expectations at 4.6%.

Third, Houthis launched a fresh wave of attacks on Saudi Arabia, digging into positions on Yemen's west coast along the Red Sea. The Oman meeting that was supposed to discuss a phased Hormuz reopening was postponed "in the interests of consensus." Iran's foreign minister repeated that Hormuz will stay closed until the U.S. meets its demands.

What the 5% actually means

For two decades, the bond market functioned as a subsidy on U.S. fiscal expansion. Low yields made deficits cheap to carry, which made spending politically painless, which made deficits larger. That feedback loop just broke. At 5%, the cost of issuing new debt is higher than the economic growth rate for most scenarios. The government is now borrowing at a real loss.

The Fed is caught. Neel Kashkari said Monday he prefers "smaller rate hikes now rather than waiting" — the explicit argument for getting ahead of inflation rather than reacting to it. But every basis point the Fed adds to the policy rate feeds directly into the fiscal arithmetic. The tool becomes the transmission mechanism for the problem it is trying to solve.

This is the paradox at the center of the next 24 hours. The Fed will hike Wednesday not because the economy is strong — consumer sentiment is at 47.8, retail sales fell 0.6%, and the labor market's August bounce may be a one-month reprieve — but because inflation has not broken. Core CPI printed +0.3% month-over-month on September 11, above the 0.2% consensus. Oil is at $107. Diesel is at $6.23 a gallon, up 30% since late June. The Fed is hiking because it has no choice, and the bond market knows it.

The equity question

S&P futures fell 0.77% Sunday night. Nasdaq-100 futures down 1.79%. The Dow down 0.26%. The selling is broad — the equal-weight S&P fell 2.4% last week while the cap-weighted index dropped only 0.8%, meaning the rally has narrowed to a handful of names carrying the index. TSM fell 3.26% on chip tariff threats. Nvidia is down 6.8% from its month high.

The question for equities is whether the 10-year at 5% reprices the discount rate on future earnings enough to matter. For the AI trade — which depends on massive forward investment funded by cheap debt — the answer is yes. Semiconductor tariff Phase 2, which would extend duties to servers and data center equipment, compounds the problem. The one sector holding up the index now faces both a cost shock from tariffs and a financing shock from yields.

What comes next

The dot plot Wednesday will tell the market whether this is one hike or the beginning of a cycle. Warsh refused to submit his own dot — a deliberate signal that he will not be pinned to a path. Capital Economics projects a second hike in December. If the dot plot shows multiple hikes ahead, the 10-year will likely hold above 5% and the equity repricing accelerates. If it shows one-and-done, the yield may pull back, but the fiscal overhang remains.

Meanwhile, the Saudi pipeline has no repair timeline. Hormuz has no reopening conditions. Diesel has no ceiling. The bond market has already voted. The question is whether the rest of the market is ready for the world that 5% describes.
