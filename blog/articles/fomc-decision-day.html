The Decision — What the Fed Does When It Has No Good Options

16 September 2026

The Federal Reserve will raise rates today. That part is settled — CME FedWatch priced 92.4% for a 25-basis-point hike as of Monday night. Goldman Sachs, J.P. Morgan, HSBC, Deutsche Bank, and Morgan Stanley all forecast the increase. The question the market is actually answering is not whether the Fed will move, but what happens next — and whether the central bank has any room left to maneuver in a world that is closing its options faster than it can open them.

The context they're hiking into

Start with what surrounds this decision. The University of Michigan consumer sentiment index printed 47.8 in the September preliminary — the weakest reading since the survey began. Year-ahead inflation expectations hit 4.6%. Only 8% of respondents expect their income to outpace inflation. The Fed is about to raise borrowing costs for an electorate that has never been this pessimistic about its own finances.

Oil is at $105 a barrel. Diesel hit $6.23 a gallon — an all-time record, up 30% since late June. The Saudi East-West pipeline, which carried 4 to 5 million barrels per day as the primary Hormuz bypass, remains shut after drone strikes from Iraq's Maysan province. Yanbu terminal stocks can sustain exports for five to seven days, putting exhaustion around September 18-19. No repair timeline has been disclosed.

Diplomacy just collapsed. The Oman meeting between GCC foreign ministers and Iran's FM Araghchi — which was supposed to discuss a phased Hormuz reopening — was postponed "in the interests of consensus". Only Iraq had confirmed attendance. Bahrain refused. Saudi Arabia submitted amendments citing concerns the emerging deal could negatively affect other GCC countries. Iran's foreign minister repeated that Hormuz will not reopen until the U.S. meets its demands.

On top of all that, President Trump announced a Ukraine-Russia energy ceasefire on Truth Social — neither side confirmed it. Ukraine struck the Syzran refinery and an Ozon warehouse overnight. The "ceasefire" is a headline, not a fact.

What 25 basis points actually does

A quarter-point increase takes the federal funds rate to 3.75-4.00%. In isolation, it is modest. But the context transforms it. The Fed is hiking into a supply shock — oil above $100, diesel at records, the Saudi bypass offline — and rate hikes strengthen the dollar, which makes oil more expensive globally, which feeds back into the domestic CPI the Fed is trying to cool. The tool becomes the transmission mechanism for the problem it is trying to solve.

The fiscal backdrop makes it worse. The U.S. is carrying $40 trillion in debt and paying $1.17 trillion a year to service it. Every basis point the Fed adds to the policy rate flows directly into that arithmetic. The 10-year yield touched 5.014% on Monday — highest since 2007 — before pulling back to 4.95%. The bond market is already doing the Fed's work of tightening financial conditions, and it is doing so without a mandate for full employment.

The dot plot is the real event

The rate decision is priced. The Summary of Economic Projections and dot plot, released at the same time, is not. This is where the market will find out whether today is one hike or the beginning of a cycle.

Warsh refused to submit his own dot — a deliberate signal that he will not be pinned to a path. Capital Economics projects a second hike in December. J.P. Morgan forecasts hikes in both September and December, and raised its long-run policy rate estimate to 3.25%. If the median dot shows multiple hikes ahead, the 10-year will likely hold above 5% and the equity repricing accelerates. If it shows one-and-done, the yield may pull back, but the fiscal overhang remains.

The three hawkish FOMC members — Kashkari, Hammack, and Logan — reinforced the case Monday. Kashkari was explicit: "prefer smaller rate hikes now rather than waiting." ING's framing captures the shift: Warsh moved the default from "hold unless data justifies hike" to "hike unless data justifies hold." The burden of proof flipped. Today is the first execution of that framing.

What's really at stake

Markets have been conditioned to expect the Fed to ease at the first sign of economic weakness. That reflex is about to be tested. Consumer sentiment is at 47.8. Retail sales fell 0.6%. The labor market's August bounce (+162K) may be a one-month reprieve. But core CPI printed +0.3% month-over-month, above the 0.2% consensus. The Fed cannot cut without abandoning its inflation credibility, and it cannot hike without deepening the squeeze on a consumer that has already cracked.

Warsh's Jackson Hole framing made this explicit: no forward guidance, no reaction function, just an open door to hikes and a statement that inflation and yields are a single challenge. The market heard hikes. The bond market is pricing a world where the government cannot stimulate and the Fed cannot cut. Today's dot plot will tell us how long that world is expected to last.

At 2:00 PM, the Fed will raise rates by 25 basis points. At 2:30, Warsh will face the press. The numbers will matter. The framing will matter more. The question is whether the Fed just hiked once into a crisis, or whether it just told the market to get ready for more.
