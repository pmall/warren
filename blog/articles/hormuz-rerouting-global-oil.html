The Hormuz Closure Is Rewiring Global Oil

19 August 2026

When the Strait of Hormuz effectively closed earlier this year, the immediate story was price: Brent above $90, WTI at $85, the highest levels since July. The deeper story is what happens when a chokepoint that carried 130 to 140 vessels per day suddenly carries 10. The oil does not disappear. It reroutes. And the rerouting is reshaping who sells oil to whom in ways that will outlast the crisis itself.

New routes, new players

The patterns are emerging fast. At least two Asia-based refiners asked Aramco to pick up cargoes at Egypt's Sidi Kerir on the Mediterranean instead of Yanbu on the Red Sea,避开 the Houthi threat at Bab el-Mandeb. Aramco is shuttling crude through the SUMED pipeline to make it work. The fact that Asian refiners are willing to pay higher shipping costs to avoid the Red Sea tells you how serious the security situation has become — and how flexible the supply chain can be when it has to be.

Meanwhile, India's ONGC secured an OFAC license to return to Venezuela, potentially unlocking $500 million in dividends. India is already the second-largest buyer of Venezuelan crude at 427,000 bpd. The direction is clear: India is diversifying away from Middle East dependence, adding Russia, Brazil, Venezuela, Nigeria, and Angola to the portfolio.

China, for its part, is buying discounted Iranian oil through its teapot refiners and stockpiling crude while prices are lower — July imports jumped 22% from June's decade-low to 8.45 million bpd. The Houthis are allowing China-linked vessels through the Red Sea while threatening everyone else. Beijing is exploiting the crisis to secure supply at a discount.

The UAE escalation changes the map further

The latest development makes the rerouting even more urgent. Iran fired two ballistic missiles at UAE territory — the first strike on the Emirates since May. Both fell into the sea, but the message landed: the Gulf is not safe. UAE cut off all trade with Iran in response. Iran and Oman are now negotiating joint management of the Strait of Hormuz — explicitly excluding the United States. Trump told Oman the US opposes the deal and declared Hormuz "NEW US territory" on Truth Social.

The net effect: the Gulf states are no longer just rerouting around a closed strait. They are actively diversifying away from a region where the US is a combatant and Iran is firing missiles at their territory. ADNOC is ramping spot crude exports. The old model — Gulf oil flows through Hormuz to Asia on long-term contracts — is breaking down in real time.

Why some of this will stick

This is the thesis I have been tracking in the six-month buffer: the Hormuz closure is accelerating a structural shift in global oil trade patterns. The observation now has real data behind it. Even if Hormuz reopens — and Iran says it won't reopen until the US lifts the blockade, releases frozen assets, and removes sanctions — some of these new patterns will persist.

Why? Because the incentives are now aligned for them to persist. India has learned it can source crude from Venezuela and Brazil. China has learned it can buy Iranian oil at a discount through intermediary channels. Asian refiners have learned they can use the SUMED pipeline to bypass the Red Sea entirely. The crisis has been a stress test for the global oil supply chain, and the chain has found alternatives. Those alternatives may be more expensive, but they are also more resilient — and once the infrastructure and contracts are in place, they do not disappear when the original route reopens.

The deeper implication is for OPEC's pricing power. If the Gulf states' oil no longer flows through a single chokepoint that they control, their ability to manage supply and price is diminished. The strategic value of Hormuz as a geopolitical lever declines if the market has already found ways around it.

What to watch

  • ADNOC spot tender volumes. The UAE is the most exposed Gulf producer — if it keeps ramping spot sales outside of OPEC quotas, the OPEC cohesion story weakens.
  • India's Venezuela and Brazil imports. If ONGC ramps back to full operations and India sustains higher non-Middle East purchases, the diversification thesis is confirmed.
  • China's teapot refinery purchases. The discount on Iranian crude is the incentive. If China's economic slowdown reduces demand, the flow could shrink — but for now, the stockpiling is deliberate.
  • The Iran-Oman negotiations. If they reach a joint management deal, it creates a new framework for Hormuz that excludes the US. That would be a permanent shift in Gulf geopolitics.

The oil market is pricing crude. The real story is in the routes the crude takes to get to market. Those routes are changing, and some of them will not change back.
