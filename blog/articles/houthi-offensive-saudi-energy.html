The Second Front — Houthis Take the War to Saudi Arabia

9 September 2026

On September 8, Yemen's Iran-aligned Houthis launched what they called a "broad offensive" against Saudi Arabia — attacking four southern cities with ballistic missiles and drones. Abha, Khamis Mushait, Jazan, and Najran were all hit. Seventy-three people were wounded. Energy facilities caught fire and operations temporarily halted. The Saudi-led coalition vowed a "firm response."

The Jazan refinery — 400,000 barrels per day of capacity, already shut from a previous attack — was targeted again. NASA satellite imagery showed large black smoke plumes from the Abha bulk plant and Jazan sites. Brent crude rose a dollar to $98 per barrel after the statement. WTI jumped more than two dollars to $93.65.

This is the moment the energy war expanded from a chokepoint to a producer.

From Maritime Disruption to Direct Strikes

For seven months, the Hormuz crisis has been defined by shipping — vessels blocked, tankers struck, exclusion zones declared. The Houthis contributed by threatening Bab el-Mandeb, a second maritime chokepoint, and by attacking tankers carrying Saudi crude through the Red Sea. But the target was always the supply chain, not the source.

Sep 8 changed that. The Houthis are now striking Saudi energy infrastructure directly. The Jazan refinery is in the southwestern corner of Saudi Arabia, near the Yemen border. It processes heavy crude from the Shaybah and Khurais fields. It has been targeted repeatedly — a pattern that suggests a deliberate campaign to shut down Saudi southern energy capacity, not a one-off retaliation.

The Houthi military spokesperson described it as a "broad offensive" against the OPEC leader. That framing matters. This is not a side skirmish in a maritime war. It is a direct assault on the world's swing producer, timed to coincide with the Iran-Hormuz exclusion zone taking shape.

The Dual-Front Energy War

The strategic picture is now clear: Iran controls the strait. The Houthis hit the producer. Together, they create a vise on global oil supply that neither chokepoint alone could achieve.

Iran's exclusion zone — the regulatory framework declared on September 7 — governs what passes through Hormuz. The Oman corridor, in its "final stage" per IRNA, creates a managed transit lane that gives Iran revenue while keeping the broader market disrupted. The strait carries roughly 7% of pre-war throughput.

Simultaneously, the Houthis are degrading Saudi production capacity from the south. Jazan refinery's 400,000 barrels per day of capacity is already offline. Saudi crude exports fell to roughly 3 million barrels per day in August — a nine-year low — as both Red Sea and Persian Gulf routes face simultaneous pressure.

The result is a dual-front squeeze. Hormuz restricts what leaves the Gulf. Houthi attacks degrade what Saudi Arabia produces. The market is pricing both risks: Brent near $98, Goldman Sachs sees potential upside to $120 if disruptions persist.

What It Means for the Market

Brent crude is up 46% year-over-year. It gained 7.8% last week alone. WTI advanced roughly 10%. The rally is no longer just about Hormuz — it is pricing the expansion of the conflict to include direct attacks on the largest oil exporter in the world.

Goldman estimates the oil market deficit at roughly 1 million barrels per day. The Houthi escalation adds a new variable: not just supply disruption, but supply destruction. Refineries shut down are harder to restart than tankers rerouted. Jazan's 400,000 barrels per day of capacity offline is a direct hit on global refining throughput, joining the roughly 5 million barrels per day already shut from Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries and Iranian disruptions.

The diesel crisis compounds the pressure. US diesel sits at a record $5.85 per gallon. Crack spreads remain near all-time highs. Heating season begins next month. Every barrel of refinery capacity lost to Houthi strikes tightens a market that was already the tightest since the 1970s.

The Political Layer

The Saudi-led coalition's vow of a "firm response" is the diplomatic minimum. But the kingdom is constrained. A direct military response against Yemen risks escalation with Iran — the Houthis' patron — at a moment when the Hormuz crisis is already at its most volatile. Saudi Arabia needs the strait open more than it needs revenge.

Qatar's foreign ministry spokesperson warned of an "industrial catastrophe" if the Hormuz crisis persists. Qatar's prime minister conducted emergency talks in Beijing with Chinese foreign minister Wang Yi on leveraging global economic weight to ensure freedom of navigation. The diplomatic infrastructure is building around the reality that Hormuz will not reopen on anyone's terms but Iran's.

For Saudi Arabia, the Houthi offensive is a reminder that the kingdom's energy infrastructure is now a frontline in a regional war. The Jazan refinery was already damaged. Four cities were hit in a single day. The "firm response" will come — but the vulnerability has been exposed, and it will not be repaired by rhetoric.

The Week Ahead

CPI on September 10 is the only variable that can shift the Fed's trajectory. Polymarket prices a September hike at 58 to 60 percent. A hot print could push Brent through $100 not on supply fears alone, but on the inflationary impulse that higher energy prices deliver into an economy already running at 3.4% CPI.

The Houthi offensive changes the calculus. The Fed was already debating whether to hike into a supply shock. Now the supply shock is getting worse — not because Hormuz is tightening, but because the producer itself is under attack. The dual-front war makes the inflation problem harder to solve and the economic slowdown harder to contain.

Goldman's $120 upside scenario is no longer a tail risk. It is the base case if the Houthi campaign continues and the Hormuz exclusion zone holds through winter.
