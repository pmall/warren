The Phased Approach — Iran and Oman Talk Terms at Hormuz

26 August 2026

Omani Foreign Minister Badr Albusaidi met Iran's Abbas Araghchi in Tehran on August 25 to discuss a "phased approach" to managing ship traffic through the Strait of Hormuz. The language is vague. The market's reaction was not. WTI crude crashed to $80.98, down 5.01% on the day — the sharpest single-day drop in weeks. The market just priced the first real possibility of a deal since the MOU expired on August 17.

What "phased approach" means

Iran rejected Oman's earlier proposal for a 50-50 split of Hormuz control on July 31, insisting on full sovereignty over the strait. The new language — "phased approach to managing ship traffic" — is different. It implies a sequence: something opens first, something else opens later, with conditions attached at each stage. That is not a reopening. It is a negotiation over the terms of a reopening.

The distinction matters because it is the first diplomatic framework that acknowledges reality. Hormuz is not going to snap back to its pre-war state of 130 to 140 transits per day. The blockade has held for 175 days. Trade patterns have rerouted. Insurance markets have repriced. A "phased approach" is an admission that whatever comes next will be built on top of the disruption, not instead of it.

Why now

The timing is not accidental. Bessent's "economic D-Day" — the "single greatest financial offensive ever" targeting oil smuggling, swap lines, cash transfers, and ship registries — is biting. Iran's rial broke 2 million per dollar, losing 7% in a single week. Iran's loadings have fallen to roughly 287,000 barrels per day, down from 2 million before the war. The economic pressure is real, and Tehran knows it.

But the pressure is not one-directional. The blockade at 70 ships — 6 transits in 24 hours versus roughly 130 per day pre-war — is inflicting costs on everyone. Gulf producers are rerouting. Asian refiners are scrambling for alternatives. The global diesel crack spread, while down from its $102 record on August 17, remains at $93 to $94 — "absolutely unprecedented". Every day the strait stays shut, the costs compound on both sides.

The selective reopening precedent

The August 24 article in this space described Iran's decision to let Iraqi tankers through as proof of concept for a controlled filter. The Albusaidi-Araghchi meeting takes that one step further. A filter is unilateral — Iran decides who passes. A "phased approach" implies bilateral agreement on the terms. That is a fundamentally different proposition.

Oman is the right interlocutor for this. Muscat has maintained diplomatic channels with Tehran throughout the crisis. Albusaidi met Araghchi in Tehran on August 25 — the most active diplomacy since the June MOU collapsed. Oman's earlier 50-50 plan was rejected, but the fact that Iran is back at the table with a new framing suggests the underlying concept — some form of managed traffic — remains alive.

What the oil price is telling you

The 5% crash in WTI is the market's verdict: this is the most significant diplomatic signal since the closure began. Not because a deal is imminent — Polymarket still puts the odds of Hormuz returning to normal before August 31 at less than 1 percent. But because a "phased approach" is the most likely eventual resolution, and the market had not priced it until today.

The prior assumption was binary: Hormuz stays shut until some grand bargain reopens it. The new assumption is sequential: partial openings, negotiated incrementally, with Iran extracting concessions at each stage. That means the supply disruption eases gradually rather than all at once — and it means oil prices decline in stages, with each phase subject to renegotiation risk.

The risks to the thesis

Iran's parliament approved transit service fees for "authorised" ships — the first known fee regime on Hormuz traffic. Forty-six tankers have been threatened with fines and confiscation. Tehran is simultaneously negotiating a phased opening and imposing a tax on any traffic that passes. That is not contradiction — it is leverage. Iran is building the fee structure now so that when the phased approach takes effect, the revenue stream is already in place.

The other risk is that "phased approach" is diplomatic language for "we are not ready to deal." Araghchi has said repeatedly that Tehran is only interested in a deal to fully end the war, not a ceasefire. If the phased approach is a stepping stone to full reopening, Iran will demand concessions — sanctions relief, prisoner exchanges, recognition of transit fees — that the US may not be willing to grant. The diplomacy could stall at phase one.

Then there is the Bessent factor. The "economic D-Day" is not paused for diplomacy. The US is simultaneously negotiating and strangling. If the sanctions campaign continues to escalate while the talks proceed, Tehran may conclude that the phased approach is a trap — a way to extract concessions without commitment. The pace of the diplomacy matters as much as its content.

What comes next

Watch for three signals. First, whether Oman publishes any framework or joint statement after the Tehran meeting — the absence of one would suggest the talks were exploratory, not substantive. Second, whether Iran allows additional nationalities through Hormuz in the coming days — a second selective reopening would confirm the filter is expanding. Third, whether Bessent's sanctions campaign pauses or accelerates — the answer tells you whether Washington is backing the diplomacy or undermining it.

The most likely outcome is a managed filtering arrangement that gradually eases supply constraints while preserving Iran's leverage over specific countries. That is not a reopening. It is a controlled decompression — slower than markets want, faster than the worst-case scenarios feared, and entirely on Iran's terms. The phased approach just became the baseline scenario for Hormuz.
