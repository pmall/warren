The One Variable — Why CPI Sep 10 Is Everything After the NFP Shock

7 September 2026

August nonfarm payrolls came in at +162K — nearly triple the +56K consensus. July was revised up from -23K to +21K, June to +31K. Net revisions added 55K jobs. The unemployment rate held at 4.1%. Participation rebounded to 61.6%. Every measure of labor market weakness that had built up over the summer — the first negative payroll since the pandemic, the lowest participation since 1976 outside Covid — reversed in a single report.

Capital Economics summed it up: "Even the most committed dove would struggle to find anything to justify keeping rates unchanged." The employment side of the Fed's dual mandate just exited the conversation. What remains is a single question: is inflation coming down fast enough to justify a hold?

Warsh's Framework Made This Inevitable

At Jackson Hole on August 28, Fed chair Kevin Warsh delivered a keynote that opened the door to rate hikes without providing forward guidance or a reaction function. The framing was deliberate: no roadmap, no commitment, just an explicit signal that hikes were on the table.

ING's reframing captured the shift precisely: Warsh moved from "hold unless data justifies hike" to "hike unless data justifies hold." That is a change in the default, not just the range of outcomes. The burden is now on the data to prevent a hike, not to cause one.

Three July dissenters — Hammack, Kashkari, and Logan — already wanted a 25bp increase. After +162K payrolls, the internal pressure intensifies. Warsh explicitly opened the door; after a print this strong, refusing to walk through it would read as inconsistency from a chair who staked credibility on data-dependence.

What CPI Has to Show

The August CPI report drops September 10, with PPI on September 11. LongForecast estimates August CPI at 3.36%. Cleveland Fed's inflation nowcast puts it at 3.38%. The target is 2%.

The gap is not closing fast enough. PCE eased to 3.7% from 4.1% — progress, but still nearly double target. Core CPI has been sticky. Oil prices up 46% year-over-year feed directly into the goods pipeline. Diesel at $5.85/gallon — all-time record — will show up in transportation costs, shelf prices, and eventually services.

For the hawks, any print above 3.2% confirms the inflation picture hasn't improved enough to justify holding. For the doves, a miss below 3.2% — especially a sharp deceleration in core — gives them cover. But the bar is high: one benign CPI print against a backdrop of +162K payrolls, record diesel, and a chair who explicitly told markets to expect hikes is not obviously enough.

The Prediction Market Split

CME FedWatch prices a September hike at ~65%. Kalshi shows 54% hold vs 45% hike. The divergence is notable. Futures markets are driven by positioning and hedging flows; prediction markets are driven by directional conviction. The gap suggests that while the institutional consensus has shifted hard toward a hike, a meaningful share of the market still expects the Fed to flinch.

Trump cleared the political road: "I have a lot of respect for him, he'll do what he has to do." Treasury Secretary Bessent argued "you don't raise into a supply shock" — the strongest public pushback from inside the administration. Governor Barr said the Fed "should act decisively" if inflation doesn't moderate. The political crosswinds are real, but Warsh has shown no signal of being influenced by them.

Why This Matters Beyond September

A September hike would be Warsh's first as chair — a signal that the Fed under new leadership is willing to act against the prevailing market consensus. The bond market has already repriced: 10-year yield at 4.78%, 2-year at 4.425% (highest since January 2025), 30-year at 5.275%. Markets are pricing rates near 4% through 2030.

The fiscal picture constrains whatever the Fed does. $1.17 trillion in annual debt servicing on $39.9 trillion debt. No rate cut for five years, per futures markets. A hike now would push borrowing costs higher into an economy where consumer sentiment sits at 51.0, retail sales fell 0.6%, and the SPR is below 300 million barrels.

But Warsh's entire Jackson Hole framing was about refusing to let the Fed be trapped by fiscal concerns or market expectations. He delivered no forward guidance precisely because he wants markets to price data, not promises. CPI on September 10 is the first test of whether that approach holds — or whether the weight of a slowing economy, a divided FOMC, and a global energy crisis forces the chair to find a reason to wait.

One print. One variable. Everything else has been taken off the table.
