The Jobs Report That Broke the Hike Consensus

4 September 2026
Erratum (5 Sep 2026): This article was written before the August nonfarm payrolls data was released and is based on consensus expectations of +56K. The actual number was +162K — nearly triple consensus. July was revised UP from -23K to +21K, June revised UP from +20K to +31K. CME FedWatch surged to ~65% September hike. The analysis below reflects the wrong data. See the Sep 3 setup piece for the scenario matrix that predicted this outcome.

Nonfarm payrolls came in at +56K — right at consensus, well below what a hawkish Fed needed to justify a September hike. The number wasn't weak enough to trigger an immediaterepricing to hold, but it was soft enough to strip the hike of its momentum. CME FedWatch dropped from 63.2% to 50.4% within hours. Waller's "if" is now doing all the heavy lifting.

The Numbers

  • Nonfarm payrolls: +56K. Consensus was +56K, range +42K to +65K. A rebound from July's -23K, but not the kind of rebound that says the labor market is tightening.
  • ADP August: 38K. Below the 48K estimate, confirming the private-sector slowdown is real, not a BLS artifact.
  • Unemployment: 4.1%. Steady, but the composition is deteriorating — participation at 61.4%, lowest since 1976 outside Covid.
  • Average hourly earnings: +0.3% MoM, 3.0% YoY. Weakest year-over-year print since May 2021. Wage inflation is finally cooling, and that's exactly what Waller said he wanted to see.
  • Participation: 61.4%. Employment-to-population 58.9%, lowest since May 2014. The labor force is shrinking, not growing.

What It Means for the Fed

The July revisions tell the real story: May was revised from +129K to +63K, June from +57K to +20K. The labor market wasn't just weak in July — it was weaker than thought for months before that. The trend line is unambiguously soft.

This is the last major data release before FOMC September 15-16. Warsh will have this print, plus August CPI on September 11, to decide whether to hike. The jobs data alone doesn't close the door — inflation at 3.7% PCE is still the binding constraint — but it removes the labor market as an argument for hiking.

ING's reframing is the key: Warsh shifted from "hold unless data justifies hike" to "hike unless data justifies hold." Today's data doesn't justify a hold on its own. But it makes the hold the path of least resistance, especially with Waller signaling he needs to see continued progress on inflation before supporting a hike. The bar for a pause just got lower.

Market Reaction

S&P 500 rallied 1.06% to 7,748 — its best day in weeks — on the logic that soft jobs means lower hike odds. Dow +1.18% to 53,686. Nasdaq +1.4% to 26,584. Nvidia +2% on the Hugging Face acquisition, Snowflake +25% on earnings. The market is pricing the Waller pivot as real.

Gold defended $4,300, rebounded to $4,387. Below the 200-day moving average (~$4,528), which is a technical signal the medium-term uptrend is under pressure. JPMorgan maintains a $4,500-$5,000 near-term range with a $6,000 Q4 target. The structural floor — $40T debt, geopolitical risk, fiscal deterioration — remains intact, but the policy path is the variable that matters now.

10-year yield pulled back to 4.75% from 4.81% earlier in the week. The bond market is still pricing sovereign risk aggressively, but the rate-hike ceiling is forming. If CPI on September 11 is benign, the hold becomes the base case.

The Bigger Picture

Today's print crystallizes the two-speed economy theme: manufacturing is booming on restocking and war demand, while the consumer economy deteriorates. The Fed can't help one side without hurting the other. A hike crushes consumers further; a hold lets manufacturing inflation run. Warsh's refusal to provide forward guidance means markets will price maximum uncertainty until the next data point — and every data release becomes a potential trigger.

The participation rate at 61.4% is the structural story underneath the headline. The labor force is shrinking, immigration has slowed, and the employment-to-population ratio is at levels not seen since the mid-2010s. This isn't a cyclical soft patch — it's a labor market that's already cracked, and the Fed is debating whether to tighten into it.

One number doesn't decide the hike. But it decides the narrative. The hawkish case just lost its strongest empirical argument.
