The Jobs Report That Decides the Fed

3 September 2026

Kevin Warsh opened the door to rate hikes at Jackson Hole. He described inflation and bond yields as a single challenge, called financial conditions "not broadly restrictive," and told markets "we have work to do." He refused to provide forward guidance or a reaction function — a deliberate choice to keep maximum uncertainty at every data release. Now his credibility as chair depends on what the Bureau of Labor Statistics reports Friday morning at 8:30 a.m.

The Setup: 66% vs. 48%

CME FedWatch prices a September hike at 65-68%, up from roughly 36% before Jackson Hole. Prediction markets diverge sharply — Polymarket and Kalshi both price the hold at 48-52%. That split means one repricing is coming, and it will be large. A strong nonfarm payrolls print — anything in the 6-figure range — makes 66% look conservative. A second consecutive negative print buries the hike entirely and revives hard-landing talk.

What the Market Already Knows

August ADP came in at 38,000, below the 47,000 consensus. Manufacturing shed 17,000. July nonfarm payrolls were -23,000 — the first decline since the pandemic. Participation sits at 61.4%, the lowest since 1976 outside of Covid. The employment-to-population ratio is 58.9%, the lowest since May 2014.

The labor market is cracking at the same time inflation remains stubbornly above target. PCE eased to 3.7% from 4.1%, but it's still nearly double the Fed's 2% goal. The University of Michigan consumer sentiment index plunged to 51.0 — only 8% of consumers expect income to outpace inflation. Year-ahead inflation expectations jumped to 4.3%.

Why This Report Matters More Than Any Other

The September FOMC meeting is Sep 15-16. The August CPI arrives around Sep 10. That leaves this Friday's payroll print as the last major labor market data point before the committee votes. Warsh has explicitly refused to telegraph his decision — "committed to a discipline, not to a decision" — so every data point carries outsized weight.

The scenario matrix is binary:

  • Negative print or unemployment at 4.3%+. September hike repriced to sub-40%. The hold becomes the base case. Warsh's Jackson Hole rhetoric looks like overkill.
  • 6-figure rebound (100K+). September hike repriced above 75%. The three dissenters in the July vote — Hammack, Kashkari, Logan — look prescient. Warsh's hand strengthens.
  • Muddle-through (40K-65K). The ambiguous case. Barclays forecasts two hikes this year (Sep and Dec). A soft-but-positive print gives Warsh room to hike if he wants to, or to wait for CPI — but his credibility depends on consistency with his own rhetoric.

The Credibility Bind

Warsh's problem is that he has talked himself into a corner. At Jackson Hole he said financial conditions are "not broadly restrictive," inflation at 3.7% PCE is "concerning," and the Fed has "work to do." He explicitly opened the door to hikes. If he then holds in September — after all that signaling — markets will read it as either inconsistency or a policy mistake.

Barclays forecasts two hikes. JPMorgan's Kelly says "markets may have been premature" and sees "little in the labor market to suggest inflationary trouble ahead." But Warsh isn't Jackson Kelly's client — he's the chair who just told the market to expect hikes. ING warns he risks "injecting yet more volatility into an already febrile bond market."

The Bond Market Is Already Pricing It In

The 10-year yield hit 4.818% intraday Tuesday — the highest since November 2023. The 30-year sits at 5.26%. Markets are pricing no rate cut for five years. The bond market is repricing sovereign risk globally, and Warsh's speech accelerated the repricing by making clear the Fed will not rescue the market with cuts. Bond yields now exceed equity earnings yields for the first time in two decades.

Friday's report will either validate the bond market's hawkish repricing or force a correction. A weak print would send the 10-year sharply lower and question whether the entire move since Jackson Hole was premature. A strong print extends it.

The Stakes

This is the first real test of whether Warsh's "quieter Fed" philosophy can survive contact with markets pricing maximum uncertainty. One report — one number — determines whether the new Fed chair hikes for the first time in this cycle, or whether his Jackson Hole rhetoric was a bluff the market called.

The ADP miss already weakened the case. A Friday miss seals it. A rebound strengthens it. There is no muddle-through that preserves Warsh's credibility without a hike — the signal he sent was too explicit for ambiguity.

Friday, 8:30 a.m. The market will know within minutes what kind of Fed chair Warsh is.
