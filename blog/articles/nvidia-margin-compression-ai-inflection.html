Signal & Noise

Nvidia's Margin Compression — The AI Capex Cycle Hits Its Limits

27 August 2026

Nvidia reported second-quarter fiscal 2027 earnings on August 26 that should have been a celebration. Revenue hit $96.2 billion, up 106% year-over-year, beating consensus by $4 billion. EPS came in at $2.22, trouncing the $2.09 estimate. Data Center revenue — the line that matters — reached $89 billion, up 117%. The company guided $108 billion for Q3, above the $104 billion Street expected. Full-year fiscal 2028 growth was projected at roughly 70%, far ahead of the 44% analysts had modeled.

The stock fell 1.6% after hours.

The market's verdict

The muted reaction to a beat of this magnitude is the signal. For the past two years, any Nvidia earnings surprise of this scale would have sent the stock up 8 to 12 percent. Instead, investors sold. The reason is not the top line — it is the trajectory of margins, and what that trajectory says about the physics of the AI buildout.

CFO Colette Kress warned that memory scarcity is compressing gross margins in a way that is structural, not cyclical. Q3 guidance came in at 74%, down from 75% in Q2. Q4 is expected to trough at 71 to 72%. And fiscal 2028 — the year of maximum growth — will settle at 72 to 73%, well below the peak. Kress was explicit: "tighter memory supply is a symptom of the same demand surge that is driving our own growth." The bottleneck is not easing through fiscal 2028.

What memory scarcity actually means

Memory — specifically high-bandwidth memory (HBM) — is the constraining input in AI hardware. Every Nvidia GPU needs HBM stacks. Every data center buildout consumes them. And the supply chain for advanced memory is dominated by a handful of producers in South Korea and Taiwan who are themselves capacity-constrained.

This is not a problem that more capital can solve quickly. HBM fabrication requires extreme ultraviolet lithography tools from ASML, which themselves have a two-year order backlog. The memory fabs being built today will not produce volume until 2028 at the earliest. Until then, every incremental GPU Nvidia ships requires memory that someone else was also going to use.

The result is a direct cost squeeze. Memory prices are rising because demand from Nvidia's own growth is driving the shortage that raises Nvidia's costs. The AI buildout is consuming the inputs it needs to scale. This is the first clear signal that the infrastructure cycle is hitting hard physical limits.

The inflection point for the AI trade

For two years, the AI trade operated on a simple premise: growth at any price. Nvidia's revenue was growing so fast that margin compression barely mattered — the volume more than compensated. That premise just broke. The market is no longer reflexively rewarding earnings beats of this scale, which means the equity rally's main engine may be losing power.

Nvidia now represents 7.94% of the S&P 500. Its mechanical index contribution drives roughly 0.43 percentage points of index movement on any given day. When a stock that large falls on a beat, the drag is not confined to semiconductors — it flows through the entire index weighting.

The question the market is now asking is not "how fast is Nvidia growing?" It is "can growth justify these margins?" At 71 to 72% gross margins versus 75% today, the profit dollars per unit are declining even as volume accelerates. The market is pricing the transition from "growth at any price" to "growth that justifies its cost structure." That is a fundamentally different valuation framework.

What it means for the capex cycle

Nvidia's margins are a proxy for the entire AI infrastructure buildout. If memory is the bottleneck that compresses Nvidia's margins, it is also the bottleneck that constrains every hyperscaler building data centers. Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Meta are all competing for the same memory supply. The cost of building AI infrastructure is rising not because of Nvidia's pricing power, but because of the physical constraints of the supply chain underneath it.

This has implications beyond semiconductors. The AI capex cycle has been the primary driver of corporate bond issuance this year — an estimated $1.5 trillion. If the returns on that capex are compressed by rising input costs, the credit risk changes. Hyperscalers are borrowing at 5%+ yields to build infrastructure whose margins are being squeezed by the same demand that justified the buildout. The feedback loop is beginning to close.

The two scenarios

The bull case is that memory supply expands in fiscal 2029 as new fabs come online, margins recover to 74 to 75%, and Nvidia's volume growth compounds into a much larger profit base. The bear case is that memory costs stay elevated, margins settle permanently lower, and the market re-rates Nvidia from a premium growth stock to a cyclical hardware company trading at a multiple that reflects commodity-like margin dynamics.

The after-hours reaction suggests the market is now pricing the bear case as at least plausible. That is new. For the first time, a massive Nvidia beat did not drive the stock higher. The AI trade just found its ceiling — not in demand, but in the physical world that supplies it.
