The Quotas That Don't Exist — OPEC+ Keeps Raising Targets Nobody Meets

6 September 2026

OPEC+ met on Sunday and did what everyone expected: nothing. The September 188K barrel-per-day increase — completing the rollback of 1.65 million barrels of voluntary cuts from 2023 — was already agreed in August. The statement made no reference to Q4 2026 output policy. The market shrugged.

But the real story isn't what OPEC+ decided. It's that what OPEC+ decides barely matters anymore. Every quota increase the group announces looks significant on paper. In practice, actual production has consistently lagged the targets — and the gap between the two is widening.

The Paper Supply

Six consecutive monthly increases since March. The 1.65 million barrel rollback is now complete on paper. Add the 2 million barrel layer of 2022 cuts still in place through year-end, and OPEC+ has an elaborate architecture of targets and phases that looks like a group carefully managing supply back to market.

But the quotas assume functioning export routes. They assume Russian refineries aren't on fire. They assume Kazakhstan's pipeline isn't disrupted. They assume tankers can leave the Persian Gulf without being struck by missiles. None of those assumptions hold.

The Physical Reality

Export disruptions in the Gulf, Russia, and Kazakhstan have broken the link between targets and supply. The group announces a 188K bpd increase; the market receives something less, because the infrastructure to deliver that crude doesn't exist in its pre-war form.

Hormuz carried 4.9 million barrels per day in Q2 2026 — down 77% from late 2025. Iraqi exports rose to 2.34 million bpd in August from 1.35 million in July, but that's one country rerouting through available terminals while the broader network stays constrained. Russian output is hampered by Ukrainian drone strikes that have hit seven of the country's largest refineries and key export facilities like Novatek's Ust-Luga terminal.

The result is a market where quota headlines matter less than physical flows. Traders know this. That's why Brent moved on Kharg Island missile strikes and OPEC+ statements barely register.

The Q4 Void

The most revealing part of Sunday's meeting wasn't what was said — it was what wasn't. The statement made no reference to Q4 2026 output policy. The next phase of the rollout is completely unresolved.

Rystad Energy's Jorge Leon called it: the base case is a Q4 pause while the group prepares for 2027 quota negotiations. DeGolyer and MacNaughton's capacity review, due at the end of September, will set the 2027 baselines — and that's where the real negotiating begins. Iraq is pushing for higher quotas. Venezuela is still weighing OPEC exit. The cartel's internal politics are as fractured as its supply chain.

What This Means

OPEC+ has become a signaling mechanism, not a supply manager. The group announces increases that look like discipline and gradualism. The market receives whatever physical infrastructure allows. The gap between the two is the true measure of the oil market's constraint — and it's a gap that missile strikes, refinery fires, and sanctions can widen at any time.

For anyone trying to price oil, the relevant question isn't what OPEC+ decided on Sunday. It's how many barrels actually leave the Persian Gulf, Russian Baltic terminals, and Kazakh pipelines this week. The rest is noise.
