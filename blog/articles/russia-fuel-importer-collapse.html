The Importer — Russia Can No Longer Fuel Itself

13 September 2026

Russia is now importing gasoline. The world's second-largest crude oil exporter — a country that shipped fuel to Europe, Asia, and the Middle East for decades — is buying refined product from South Korea, Turkey, and India. The reversal is not a projection or a warning. It is happening. A CREA report confirmed the imports in September, citing Ukrainian intelligence and trade data.

Forty percent of Russia's refining capacity is offline. Wholesale gasoline prices have surged 50% since January. Crimea is rationing fuel to 20 liters per customer. The Omsk refinery — Russia's largest at 440,000 barrels per day — halted operations after a Ukrainian drone strike. Deputy Prime Minister Novak called the situation "challenging but under control." The data says otherwise.

From exporter to buyer

Before the war, Russia exported roughly 1.5 million barrels per day of refined products — gasoline, diesel, naphtha, and fuel oil. The country was the world's second-largest product exporter behind China. European refiners depended on Russian diesel. African and Latin American economies ran on Russian gasoline.

That infrastructure is now broken. Ukrainian drones struck Russian refineries 21 times in August alone — a record month. Every major Lukoil refinery is offline. Lukoil produced roughly a quarter of Russia's gasoline. NORSI, the country's fourth-largest refinery, shut after a drone strike on August 26. Perm went down August 21. Volgograd on July 31. The campaign has moved beyond military logistics targets into the civilian fuel backbone.

Russia banned gasoline exports in February. Diesel and marine fuel exports were banned in August through September 30. Novak extended the gasoline ban through the end of the year and added a diesel ban for non-producers. Wholesale gasoline prices surged 50% since January, according to SPIMEX data. Sales fell 20% in the first half of August — not because demand collapsed, but because supply did.

Belarus fuel imports are up 141 times year-over-year. The scale of that number — not a percentage, but a multiplier — tells you how far the domestic supply has fallen. Moscow and St. Petersburg are rationing. Wikipedia now has a full article on the "2025–2026 Russian fuel crisis."

The import geometry

The countries Russia is now buying from are its geopolitical rivals or, at best, opportunistic intermediaries. South Korea, which has sanctioned Russian energy imports, is selling gasoline through intermediaries. Turkey is brokering. India — which has been buying discounted Russian crude throughout the war — is now selling refined product back to Russia. The trade flow has inverted.

The economics are absurd on their face. Russia sells crude to India at a discount. India refines it into gasoline. India sells the gasoline back to Russia at a premium. Russia is paying more for refined product than it would earn exporting the crude. The country is effectively subsidizing Indian refining margins while depleting its own strategic position.

The Omsk refinery is the clearest illustration. At 440,000 barrels per day, it was the largest single refinery in Russia. A Ukrainian drone strike forced a full halt. Restarting a refinery after a kinetic strike is not a matter of days — it requires assessment, parts procurement, and safety certification. NORSI, hit August 26, remains shut. The pattern across 21 strikes in August suggests that repair cycles are being outpaced by new strikes.

The diesel dimension

Russia's diesel crisis compounds the global supply squeeze. Russian diesel exports crashed to approximately 80,000 barrels per day — down from roughly 1 million barrels per day pre-war. The export ban through January 2027 means the Atlantic Basin diesel market, already at record crack spreads, loses one of its traditional suppliers.

US diesel hit $5.90 per gallon — an all-time record. California is at $7.70. Refiners are running at 98% utilization, effectively flat out, and distillate stocks remain 14% below the five-year average. The refining capacity offline globally — roughly 5 million barrels per day below year-ago per Enverus — is not coming back quickly. Each refinery hit by a drone is months from resumption.

The timing is the worst it could be. Heating season begins next month in the Northern Hemisphere. Agricultural demand for diesel peaks during fall harvest. The Atlantic Basin crack spread is at record levels because there is no spare capacity to absorb a disruption. If a Gulf Coast hurricane hits US refining — something Kloza has warned could push diesel above $200 per barrel — the margin would go vertical. Valero's COO estimated roughly 5 million barrels per day of global refining capacity offline from the combined Ukraine and Iran disruptions.

The political arithmetic

For the first time, the Ukrainian drone campaign is directly affecting Russian civilian life at scale. Gasoline rationing in Moscow and St. Petersburg is the kind of measure that erodes the social contract between the Kremlin and the population. Fuel shortages in the periphery — Crimea, the regions — were tolerable. In the capitals, they are not.

Zelenskyy has ordered the military to scale long-range strikes to 1,000 per day from roughly 300. If executed, September's refinery toll could surpass August's record. The question is whether Russia's air defenses — already stretched across a 1,600-kilometer front — can protect domestic infrastructure when the same systems are needed in Ukraine.

The Kremlin's response is two-fold: extend export bans to keep whatever fuel is produced inside Russia, and blame the West. Deputy PM Novak's characterization of the situation as "challenging but under control" is the standard framing. But 40% of refining capacity offline is not under control. Importing gasoline from countries you were selling it to two years ago is not under control.

What it means

Russia's fuel crisis is no longer a supply-chain disruption. It is a structural reversal. The country that was the backbone of global diesel supply is now a net buyer. The export ban, initially framed as temporary, keeps getting extended — a signal that domestic supply has not stabilized. Each extension makes the ban look less like a policy tool and more like an admission.

The global diesel market absorbs this in a specific way: the crack spread stays elevated, distillate stocks stay below average, and the price of every good that moves by truck, ship, or train stays high. This is not a Hormuz problem. It is a refining problem. Even if the strait reopens tomorrow, the damaged refineries in Russia, the offline capacity in the Middle East, and the aging global fleet do not rebuild in months.

For Russia, the fuel importer label is a reputational and strategic milestone. The country's leverage over global energy markets was built on two pillars: crude exports and refined product exports. One pillar is now supporting the other — and it is cracking.
