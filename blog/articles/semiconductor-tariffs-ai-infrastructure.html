The Tariff That Could Break the AI Rally

28 August 2026

The White House is considering sweeping new tariffs on semiconductors — and not just chips. Politico reported on August 27 that the administration is weighing tariffs on servers, laptops, and gaming consoles — anything made with a chip. Eight anonymous sources confirmed the discussion. Commerce Secretary Lutnick is tying tariff relief to US manufacturing investment. A phase-in period is being considered. The industry response was blunt: it could doom US AI dominance.

The timing is not accidental. The S&P 500 just had its narrowest rally in recorded history. On August 27, information technology was the only positive sector in the entire index — up 3.4% while everything else was flat to down. Just six Dow stocks closed green. Salesforce rose 22%, Okta 28%, CrowdStrike 20.5%, Palo Alto 13%. The entire market advance was being carried by AI and software. The rest of the economy was not participating.

A circular risk

Here is the problem. If tariffs hit servers and data center equipment, the cost of building AI infrastructure goes up — at the exact moment when Nvidia is warning that memory scarcity is already compressing margins. Gross margins are expected to trough at 71 to 72% in Q4, down from 75%, because the physical inputs that go into AI hardware are scarce. Tariffs would add another layer of cost on top of that squeeze.

The hyperscalers — Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Meta — are all building data centers at a pace that requires importing enormous volumes of servers and networking equipment. Much of that hardware is assembled in Asia. A tariff on servers is a tariff on the AI buildout itself. And the AI buildout is the only thing holding up the equity market.

This creates a feedback loop that has no good exit. Tariffs raise the cost of AI infrastructure. Higher costs compress the returns on that infrastructure. Compressed returns make the $1.5 trillion in AI-related corporate bond issuance riskier. And the one sector carrying the entire S&P 500 — the one that was the only green sector on August 27 — now has to justify its capital expenditure at a higher cost basis.

The industrial policy gambit

Lutnick's framing — tariff relief tied to US manufacturing investment — signals that this is industrial policy disguised as trade policy. The administration wants chip fabrication to happen onshore. The CHIPS Act allocated $52.7 billion for domestic semiconductor manufacturing. Tying tariff relief to investment is a way of saying: build here, or pay the tax.

The problem is that the timeline does not work. TSMC's Arizona fab is producing chips, but at volumes that are a fraction of what the AI buildout demands. Intel's Ohio fab is years from full production. Samsung's Taylor, Texas plant is still ramping. The fabs that would make domestic server production viable do not exist at scale yet. A tariff imposed now — even with a phase-in — raises costs immediately while the domestic alternative takes years to materialize.

And the global supply chain for AI hardware is not something that can be rerouted quickly. The servers that go into data centers contain components from dozens of countries. A laptop is an assembly of parts from Taiwan, South Korea, Japan, China, Vietnam, and Malaysia. Tariffing the finished product while the supply chain is globally dispersed means the cost lands on the buyer — the hyperscaler, the enterprise, the consumer — not the manufacturer.

The narrowest rally in history

The fact that the S&P 500 is being carried by a single sector is itself a warning. When a rally is this narrow — one sector carrying the entire index while nine of eleven sectors decline — it is either a rotation setup or a leadership exhaustion signal. The semiconductor tariff threat is the new risk that could tip it toward exhaustion.

Nvidia reversed from a 1.6% after-hours decline to an 8.7% gain the next day — the market decided that 70% growth justifies margin compression. That was a strong signal that the AI trade still has conviction. But conviction that the AI trade works at current margins is not the same as conviction that it works at margins compressed further by tariffs. The market cleared one bar. The tariff threat raises a second, potentially higher one.

Michael Burry warned of a 1987-style crash. The comparison may be premature, but the underlying observation — that the market's advance depends on a narrowing set of assumptions that are now under simultaneous pressure — is hard to dismiss. The AI buildout is the engine. Memory scarcity is already squeezing the engine. Tariffs on the hardware that carries the engine are the next potential brake.

What happens next

Nothing is decided. The discussions are internal. A phase-in period means the immediate impact is limited. But the signal is the point. The market is pricing an AI infrastructure cycle that depends on free-flowing global trade in chips and hardware. The administration is signaling that trade in chips and hardware will not be free-flowing. Those two prices cannot both be right.

The semiconductor tariff is not yet policy. But the fact that it is being discussed — openly, with eight sources confirming, at the moment when the equity market's entire advance depends on the thing being tariffed — means the market now has to price a tail risk it was not pricing a week ago. In a market this narrow, a new tail risk does not need to materialize to matter. It just needs to exist.
