The Emergency Buffer Is Gone

15 August 2026

The U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve fell below 300 million barrels this week for the first time since the 1980s. It is heading toward roughly 243 million barrels once the 172-million-barrel drawdown for the Iran war finishes. That number — 243 million — is a quarter of what it was before the 2022 Ukraine drawdown. The reserve that was built to cushion the country against a strategic supply shock is nearly gone, and the geopolitical environment is the most supply-constrained it has been in decades.

Two wars, four years, one reserve

The 2022 drawdown released 180 million barrels to offset the disruption from Russia's invasion of Ukraine. The 2026 drawdown added 172 million barrels for the Iran conflict. That is 352 million barrels in four years — the kind of depletion the system was never designed to handle. The SPR's salt caverns were engineered for five full drawdown-and-refill cycles over their lifetime. They have been through dozens.

Energy Department officials told the Government Accountability Office they are "holding the SPR together with Band-Aids." A Texas A&M professor who studies cavern mechanics put it more bluntly: the rapid drawdowns put "cavern integrity and overall operational capability" at elevated risk. The concern is not hypothetical — salt caverns can collapse if drawn down too quickly, and the cumulative stress of repeated cycles degrades the geological structure that holds the oil in the first place.

The buffer was needed now more than ever

The SPR exists for exactly this moment: a major supply disruption from a strategic adversary while the U.S. faces concurrent energy pressure elsewhere. The Strait of Hormuz is effectively closed. ADNOC vessels were attacked in the strait on August 13 and 14. Only two vessels transited on August 14, with no crude visible. Global seaborne petroleum product trade is projected down 3.8 million bpd. Global inventories have drawn 410 million barrels since the war began.

At the same time, the global diesel crunch is at its worst since the 1970s. U.S. diesel retail prices are at $5.32 per gallon, up from $3.71 a year ago. Refineries are running at 95-100% utilization with maintenance season approaching. There is no excess capacity to absorb another disruption.

Jake Hochstein, the former Biden-era energy coordinator, warned that a hurricane hitting the Gulf Coast at current SPR levels could put the U.S. in a dangerous position. The reserve was the insurance policy for exactly that scenario. It is no longer available.

Refilling will take years

The refill process is slow by design — the government buys crude gradually to avoid spiking prices, and the caverns need time to restabilize after deep drawdowns. At a pace of roughly 100,000 to 200,000 barrels per day of refilling — which would be aggressive — restoring 352 million barrels would take five to ten years. At a more realistic pace, it takes longer.

The fiscal picture makes it worse. The U.S. is already running a FY2026 deficit of roughly $1.8 trillion, with $1.17 trillion going to debt servicing on $39.9 trillion of national debt. There is no political appetite for spending billions to refill a reserve while the country is simultaneously fighting a war and running record deficits. The refill will compete for funding against everything else, and everything else is more urgent in the short term.

What this changes

The absence of the SPR as a meaningful buffer changes the risk calculus for every energy-dependent sector. The U.S. used to have a backstop — a guarantee that a supply shock would be met with immediate physical supply. That backstop is gone. The next disruption — whether a hurricane in the Gulf, a further escalation in the Middle East, a complete Russian production collapse, or a combination of all three — will hit the country with no strategic cushion.

Markets have not fully priced this in because the SPR still technically exists at 243 million barrels. But 243 million barrels is roughly 12 days of U.S. crude consumption. The reserve that once held enough oil to cover two months of imports is now a rounding error in a market that moves 100 million barrels a day globally.

The strategic calculus has shifted. The U.S. is simultaneously the world's largest crude producer and a country with no meaningful strategic reserve. That combination — production strength with reserve weakness — creates a vulnerability that did not exist two years ago. Every geopolitical scenario involving energy supply now starts from a weaker baseline.

What to watch

  • Cavern integrity reports. The GAO and Energy Department have been monitoring this. Any public disclosure of structural problems in the storage caverns would signal the SPR is approaching a functional floor, not just a political one.
  • Refill pace and authorization. Congressional authorization for refilling — and the budget to pay for it — are the near-term signals. Without both, the reserve stays depleted regardless of what the market does.
  • Gulf hurricane season. The peak of Atlantic hurricane season runs through October. A major storm hitting Gulf production infrastructure while the SPR is at 243 million barrels is the worst-case scenario Hochstein described.

The United States entered 2026 with a strategic reserve that could have cushioned a supply crisis. It exits the summer with the reserve depleted by two wars in four years, the salt caverns stressed beyond their design, and no realistic path to refill quickly. The emergency buffer is gone.
