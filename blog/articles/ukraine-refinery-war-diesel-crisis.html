The Refinery War — Ukraine's Campaign Against Russian Oil Turns Global

31 August 2026

In August, Ukrainian drones struck Russian oil refineries 21 times — a record month that has now pushed every major Lukoil refinery offline and triggered gasoline rationing in Moscow and St. Petersburg. The campaign has moved beyond military logistics. It is now dismantling Russia's civilian fuel supply, and the effects are bleeding into global diesel markets already stretched to their limits.

The numbers tell the story. Russian crude processing fell to approximately 3.91 million barrels per day in July, roughly 1.4 million barrels per day below year-ago levels and the lowest in roughly 20 years. The strikes have accelerated since: NORSI, Russia's fourth-largest refinery and second-largest gasoline producer, shut after a drone strike on August 26. Perm refinery went down August 21. Volgograd on July 31. All major Lukoil refineries — the company that produces roughly a quarter of Russia's gasoline — are now offline.

Rationing hits Moscow

Russia's response is the most revealing signal. Gasoline sales fell 20% in the first half of August. Rosneft is capping purchases at 30 liters per vehicle. Multiple oblasts have imposed odd-even license plate restrictions on fuel sales. Moscow and St. Petersburg — the two cities least expected to face domestic shortages — are now rationing. Belarus fuel imports are up 141 times year-over-year. Wikipedia now has a full article on the "2025–2026 Russian fuel crisis."

Zelenskyy has ordered the military to triple the daily rate of long-range strikes on Russia. The August refinery campaign was already a record. If the order is executed at scale, September could be worse.

The global diesel crunch deepens

The timing could not be worse for global diesel markets. US diesel crack spreads are at approximately $94 per barrel — down from the all-time record of $102 hit on August 17, but still roughly four times the normal range of $15 to $25. On-highway diesel is at $5.652 per gallon, the highest since July 2022. Distillate stocks stand at 103.4 million barrels, 14% below the five-year average.

The supply side is deteriorating from multiple directions. IIR Energy reports approximately 1.6 million barrels per day of Middle East refining capacity offline. Russia has banned diesel and marine fuel exports through September 30. Seaborne Russian product exports have crashed 55%. Aramco's Jazan refinery — at 400,000 barrels per day, the single largest swing factor for the coming weeks — had its restart pushed to August 30 after Houthi attacks.

The demand side is responding, but slowly. Four-week distillate demand is down 2.2% year-over-year — the first visible sign of price-driven destruction. For a typical long-haul truck, the diesel price increase adds roughly $16,300 per year in operating costs. The market has stopped clearing on price alone and started clearing on availability.

Why this is structural, not cyclical

The instinct is to treat this as a Hormuz blip that will resolve when the strait reopens. That misses the point. Even as Persian Gulf crude flows recover to 6 to 8 million barrels per day — roughly two-thirds of pre-war levels per Goldman Sachs estimates — the product squeeze persists. Crude is available. What the market lacks is the refining capacity to turn it into diesel, gasoline, and jet fuel.

The damage is slow to repair. A refinery hit by a drone is not back online in days. NORSI was hit on August 26 and remains shut. Lukoil's entire refinery network is down. Global refinery throughputs are roughly 5 million barrels per day below year-ago. Refineries are running at 97.4% utilization — effectively flat out — and output is still falling for a third straight week. There is no slack in the system.

What it means

The war's energy impact has shifted from a crude supply story — Hormuz closure, Iranian exports halted — to a refining and products story. That is harder to resolve. Crude can reroute. New trade patterns emerge. But damaged refineries take months or years to rebuild, and the global refining fleet was already aging and underinvested before the war began.

For consumers, this means diesel stays expensive relative to crude for a prolonged period. For the global economy, it means a structural cost increase on every good that moves by truck, ship, or train. For Russia, the domestic fuel shortage is becoming a political liability — the first time the Ukrainian drone campaign has directly affected Russian civilian life at scale.

The diesel crack spread is the number to watch. It pulled back from $102 to $94 on the Jazan restart expectation. If Jazan comes online smoothly and Ukrainian strikes slow, it could ease further. If strikes accelerate per Zelenskyy's order, or if a Gulf Coast hurricane disrupts US refining, the spread goes back to triple digits. BofA's assessment holds: diesel will stay tight, volatile, and expensive well into next year.
