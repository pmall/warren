The Cartel Breaks Apart — Venezuela Weighs OPEC Exit

30 August 2026

Venezuela is considering quitting OPEC. Bloomberg reported the discussions on August 28, adding that Caracas is in talks with US officials over a 100-year lease on several oil fields — part of a deal that would give American interests majority control over development rights covering more than 65 billion barrels of reserves in the Orinoco Belt and Lake Maracaibo. Trump announced the deal himself, calling it worth $100 billion in private investment. Venezuela produced 1.16 million barrels per day in July — less than half what it pumped a decade ago.

The timing is not accidental. Venezuela would be the third significant departure from OPEC's core in four months, following the UAE's exit in April over production constraints and Iraq's growing vocal dissatisfaction. Together, the UAE and Venezuela represent more than 5 million barrels per day of capacity — roughly 17% of OPEC's core capacity at the start of 2026. OPEC's share of global crude production has already dropped from above 48% before the Iran war to approximately 40% in July.

The US-Venezuela oil alliance

The proposed deal is not just a commercial arrangement. It is industrial policy disguised as diplomacy. A 100-year lease on Venezuelan oil fields, with American companies holding majority control, gives Washington a strategic supply buffer outside OPEC's framework — at the precise moment the US Strategic Petroleum Reserve sits at a four-decade low of 243 million barrels.

American officials envision a US-Venezuela oil partnership that would "greatly diminish OPEC's influence." The language is revealing. This is not about helping Venezuela develop its resources. It is about building a bilateral supply relationship that bypasses the cartel entirely — the same way the US has pursued bilateral energy deals with Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Qatar.

Why Venezuela wants out

Venezuela's production has collapsed from over 2.5 million barrels per day a decade ago to 1.16 million today. The country needs investment, technology, and market access that OPEC's production quotas cannot provide. Under OPEC's framework, Venezuela would be constrained by collective output targets — targets set largely by Saudi Arabia and Russia, whose interests do not align with Caracas's need to rebuild capacity as fast as possible.

The US deal offers something OPEC cannot: capital, technology, and a guaranteed buyer. The 100-year lease structure is designed to attract the kind of long-term private investment that Venezuela's battered oil sector has not seen in years. For Maduro's government, the calculus is straightforward — OPEC membership provides little benefit when the cartel is fracturing, production quotas limit recovery, and a bilateral deal with Washington offers a path to rebuild.

The cartel's structural decline

OPEC's problem is not just member departures. It is that the cartel's remaining members cannot agree on quotas while the institution's ability to manage supply erodes from multiple directions. The UAE left over production constraints. Iraq is grumbling. Venezuela may leave. Iran's exports are halted by a US blockade. Saudi Arabia is shuttling crude north to evade Houthi threats at Bab el-Mandeb.

The cartel's share of global crude production has fallen from above 48% to approximately 40% in less than a year. That is not a cyclical dip. It is a structural shift driven by three forces: the Iran war disrupting Middle Eastern supply, Ukrainian drone strikes destroying Russian refining capacity, and the US building bilateral oil relationships with producer nations that bypass OPEC's framework entirely.

What it means for oil markets

The immediate market impact is limited. Venezuela produces 1.16 million barrels per day — meaningful, but not enough to move global prices on its own. The significance is structural. Every bilateral deal the US signs with a producer nation is a deal that doesn't go through OPEC. Every barrel Venezuela produces under a US lease is a barrel that doesn't count toward OPEC's quota system. The cartel's ability to manage supply — its only real function — erodes with each departure.

For oil markets, this means the supply picture becomes less coordinated and more fragmented. OPEC's ability to cut production to support prices weakens. The US gains a supply buffer that doesn't depend on Saudi-Russian negotiations. And the Hormuz closure, which has already rerouted 80% of Persian Gulf crude flows, becomes less relevant to the US specifically — because Washington is building supply relationships outside the Gulf.

The broader signal

Venezuela's potential OPEC exit is the latest data point in a larger pattern: the global oil market is fragmenting along geopolitical lines, not commercial ones. The US builds bilateral deals. China buys discounted Russian and Iranian crude. India scrambles for alternatives. Gulf producers reroute around Houthis. The cartel that once managed 48% of global supply is becoming a negotiating forum for countries that can't find better deals elsewhere.

The 100-year lease is the detail that matters. A century-long arrangement is not a transaction — it is an alignment. Venezuela is choosing a side. The question is how many other OPEC members will make the same choice before the cartel's production quotas become suggestions rather than rules.
