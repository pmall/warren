# Writing style guide for prompt instructions

## Give it someone to be, not a list to follow

A persona a sentence lets you inhabit works better than a list of forbidden behaviors. "Read like a reviewer, not an auditor" changes how something approaches a whole task. "Don't verify claims, don't fact-check, don't argue with sources" is a checklist to comply with — similar content, different result. When you're tempted to write a rule, first ask if there's a role that would make the rule unnecessary.

## Earn every negation

Telling a reader what not to do still puts the wrong idea in their head — naming something to forbid it isn't free, the same way "don't think of an elephant" doesn't work. State what something is, not what it isn't. When a negation is genuinely needed, keep it tight and specific — don't let it expand into a general list of adjacent things that also sound bad.

## Say why, not just what

A rule with its reasoning attached generalizes to situations you didn't spell out. A bare rule gets followed literally, including in cases where literal compliance produces something absurd. "Semantic HTML only" is a rule. "...because that's what makes an article genuinely permanent, since the whole design lives in one swappable file" is a rule a reader can correctly extend on their own.

## Be concrete when overriding a habit

Vague, permissive phrasing ("organize however makes sense") works fine against an open decision. It's much weaker against a strong, familiar default — a common convention, a common phrase, a common shape of ending. Against a habit like that, specific wording beats gentle wording. Name the actual thing, once, plainly.

## Say each thing once

If two sentences — even worded differently — establish the same fact, cut one. Restating an idea in different words adds length, not information, and gives the two versions room to quietly drift apart in meaning over time.

## Match the strength of the wording to the strength of the rule

Soft language ("consider," "you might," "aim for") reads as optional, because it is. Hard language ("must," "always," "never") reads as absolute. Pick the one that's actually true.

## Keep one voice

Pick a register — how formal, how warm, how terse — and hold it for the whole document. A stray phrase that drifts from it, however good it sounds in isolation, weakens the rest.
